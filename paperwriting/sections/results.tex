\section{Results}

\subsection{Surprisal predicts human behavior and neural activity}
\label{sec:results-surprisal}

We first ask how well each group's word-by-word surprisal predicts human data, at the last layer, before turning to embeddings.

\paragraph{Behavioral (BehavScore).} Standard LLMs post the highest mean surprisal-track BehavScore of any group (0.113), with BabyLM close behind (0.108); both memory-constrained groups trail noticeably (Constrained-BabyLM 0.068, Memory-Constrained 0.061). Standard LLM and BabyLM are close enough here that we do not read this as a clear win for either group -- what is clear is that both groups without the attention-bounding constraint separate from both groups with it.

\paragraph{Neural (NeuralScore).} The ordering is the same but the gaps are larger: Standard LLM leads (0.112), followed by BabyLM (0.071), then Memory-Constrained (0.040) and Constrained-BabyLM (0.025). Unlike on BehavScore, Standard LLM's advantage over BabyLM here is substantial, not marginal.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto} plots both scores against each other directly. On the full set of words (Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}A) the Pareto front is occupied exclusively by three Standard LLMs -- Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B, and OPT-1.3B -- out of the 100 models with both scores available; no memory-constrained, data-constrained, or architecture-and-data-constrained model reaches the frontier at any point along it. Restricting to the syntactically hardest words (DLT integration cost $\geq 2$; Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}B and Appendix~\ref{app:dlt}) does not change this qualitative picture, although it does change which Standard LLMs make up the frontier: Mamba2-1.3B and Mamba2-2.7B anchor the high-BehavScore end, with GPT-2-medium, GPT-J-6B, and Qwen3-8B filling out the rest of the curve toward higher NeuralScore. That the frontier survives intact under a subset chosen specifically to stress incremental-parsing difficulty is, if anything, the harder test for our benchmark's central hypothesis: a psycholinguistically motivated architectural constraint, tested precisely where its motivating theory predicts it should matter most, still does not produce a single model on the Pareto front.

\subsection{Scaling with model size}
\label{sec:results-scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figure3_scaling_a}
    \\[2pt] \textbf{(A)} Surprisal features.
    \\[6pt]
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figure3_scaling_b}
    \\[2pt] \textbf{(B)} Embedding features.
    \caption{BehavScore/BrainScore as a function of model parameter count (log scale), by dataset modality and model group, last layer. \textbf{(A)} Surprisal features (DLL-ceiling-normalized). \textbf{(B)} Embedding features, expressed as fraction of correlation noise ceiling. Dashed lines are per-group linear fits in log-parameter space.}
    \label{fig:scaling}
\end{figure}

Within the Standard LLM group, larger models are, on the whole, better predictors of both human neural and behavioral responses to surprisal. A log-linear fit of surprisal-track score against parameter count is positive in four of our five dataset modalities (fMRI: $+0.051$; ECoG: $+0.065$; self-paced reading: $+0.005$; eye-tracking: $+0.018$, score units per decade of parameters), with only the N400/ERP modality essentially flat ($+0.003$) -- unsurprising given that ERP amplitude is among the noisiest and lowest-$N$ of our signals. Pooling across all eleven surprisal-track datasets that have a computable noise ceiling (Figure~\ref{fig:scaling}A), mean DLL-normalized score for Standard LLMs rises from 0.135 in our smallest bin (14--125M parameters) to roughly 0.20--0.24 across bins spanning 1B to 66B parameters; we did not test whether this trend continues, plateaus, or reverses beyond the range of models we evaluate, and do not draw a conclusion about its shape at the largest sizes. The embedding track scales more modestly across the same range but in the same direction in every modality.

The two memory-constrained groups do not show this pattern as reliably. Their own within-group scaling slopes are noisier and in some modalities negative -- Constrained-BabyLM's surprisal-track slope is negative in both fMRI ($-0.057$) and ECoG ($-0.079$), and Memory-Constrained is negative in fMRI as well ($-0.044$) -- though we note both groups span a narrower and lower parameter range than the Standard LLMs (roughly 14M to 1.3B, versus up to 66B), so these local slopes are estimated over much less dynamic range and should be read with that caveat.

\subsection{Embeddings}
\label{sec:results-embeddings}

The embedding track tells a more qualified version of the same story. On NeuralScore, the ordering matches the surprisal track: Standard LLM leads (0.130), followed by BabyLM (0.105), with Memory-Constrained and Constrained-BabyLM again trailing (0.079 and 0.077). On BehavScore, however, the four groups are close together (0.478--0.512), and BabyLM's mean is nominally the highest of the four -- a genuine difference from the surprisal track, where Standard LLM leads on both scores, but one confined to a narrow range around half of the correlation noise ceiling rather than a clear separation between groups. We do not read embedding BehavScore as evidence that any constrained group outperforms Standard LLMs overall: NeuralScore, on the same embeddings, shows the opposite ordering, and a linear ridge regression's fit is also more sensitive than a scalar surprisal predictor to a model's raw representational capacity (Appendix~\ref{app:best-layer}), which is part of why we treat the surprisal track, not the embedding track, as our primary comparison across groups (Section~3.3).

\subsection{Layer depth and layer selection}
\label{sec:results-layerwise}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figure4_layerwise_a}
    \\[2pt] \textbf{(A)} Surprisal features.
    \\[6pt]
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/figure4_layerwise_b}
    \\[2pt] \textbf{(B)} Embedding features.
    \caption{BehavScore/BrainScore as a function of relative layer depth (layer index / total layers), per dataset and model group. \textbf{(A)} Surprisal features (DLL-ceiling-normalized). \textbf{(B)} Embedding features. Shaded bands are $\pm$1 SE across models within a group.}
    \label{fig:layerwise}
\end{figure}

The two tracks look different across depth. On the surprisal track (Figure~\ref{fig:layerwise}A), most datasets show scores still rising, or at least not yet declining, at the final layer, for Standard LLM and BabyLM alike. On the embedding track (Figure~\ref{fig:layerwise}B), the pattern is instead a rise through early-to-middle depth followed by a decline toward the final layer, and this shape is common to all four groups rather than specific to any one of them -- a genuinely different depth profile from the surprisal track, not just a weaker version of it.

This motivates checking how much a last-layer summary leaves on the table relative to each model's own best layer. Averaged over all models, last-layer BehavScore recovers a large majority of best-layer BehavScore in every group and on both tracks (85--92\% on surprisal, 82--97\% on embeddings). NeuralScore recovers a smaller and more variable share: on the surprisal track this ranges from 18\% (Constrained-BabyLM) to 80\% (BabyLM), with Standard LLM at 56\%; on the embedding track, Standard LLM has the \emph{lowest} recovery fraction of the four groups (47\%, versus 49--64\% for the other three) -- consistent with the decline visible in Figure~\ref{fig:layerwise}B being, if anything, proportionally steepest for Standard LLM on this track. We do not have a settled explanation for this asymmetry (a natural candidate is that Standard LLMs simply have more layers over which a peak-then-decline profile can unfold, but we have not tested this directly). We report best-layer NeuralScore and BehavScore in full in Appendix~\ref{app:best-layer} as a secondary, exploratory upper bound; the ordering between groups established above is unchanged there, but for the reasons just given we do not treat it as the basis for any headline claim (Section~3.3).
