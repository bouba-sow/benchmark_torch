\section{Normalization of BrainScore and BehavScore}
\label{app:normalization}

Raw correlations and log-likelihood improvements are not comparable across datasets: a Pearson $r$ of 0.3 might be close to ceiling in a noisy single-trial iEEG recording and far from ceiling in a clean, heavily-averaged fMRI dataset. We therefore normalize every score by an estimate of how much signal is actually there to be predicted, using two different procedures for our two metrics, since each requires a different noise ceiling.

\subsection{Correlation ceilings: between-subject split-half reliability}
\label{app:corr-ceiling}

For every dataset where a model's score is a correlation between predicted and observed responses (Pearson or Spearman $r$, at the level of an electrode, voxel, ROI, or channel), we estimate a noise ceiling from the split-half reliability of the human data, following the correction described by \citet{vanbree2025noiseceiling}.

Our datasets typically present each item once to each participant, so there is no within-subject repetition to split on. We instead split \emph{across} subjects: for a given dataset and response unit, we repeatedly draw two random, equally sized halves of the subject pool, average each half's response per item, and correlate the two half-averages across items to obtain a split-half correlation $r_{\text{sh}}$ -- the group-average variant of split-half reliability that \citeauthor{vanbree2025noiseceiling} describe for designs without stimulus repetitions. We apply the standard Spearman-Brown correction to extrapolate from a half-sample to the full sample,
\[
r_{\mathrm{SB}} = \frac{2\,r_{\text{sh}}}{1 + r_{\text{sh}}},
\]
and average $r_{\mathrm{SB}}$ over many random subject splits to stabilize the estimate.

It is tempting to treat $r_{\mathrm{SB}}$ itself as the ceiling for a model's correlation with the data, but this is the mistake \citeauthor{vanbree2025noiseceiling} warn against: $r_{\mathrm{SB}}$ is a correlation between two noisy halves, so by the classical correction-for-attenuation logic it bounds \emph{explained variance} ($R^2$), not the correlation coefficient itself. A model that perfectly recovers the true signal is not itself noisy, so its correlation with the (still noisy) data is attenuated only once rather than twice, giving
\[
r_{\max} = \sqrt{r_{\mathrm{SB}}}.
\]
We therefore compare every model correlation against $\sqrt{r_{\mathrm{SB}}}$, and every model $R^2$ against $r_{\mathrm{SB}}$ directly, reporting a model's normalized score as the fraction of this ceiling it recovers ($r_{\text{model}} / \sqrt{r_{\mathrm{SB}}}$). Using $r_{\mathrm{SB}}$ as a ceiling for a raw correlation, as roughly sixty percent of the papers surveyed by \citeauthor{vanbree2025noiseceiling} do, would systematically understate how far a model actually is from ceiling, especially at the low-to-intermediate reliability that characterizes most of our neural datasets. Ceilings are computed separately per response unit, using the same correlation family -- Pearson or Spearman -- as the corresponding model score, so a score and its ceiling always live on the same scale.

\subsection{Delta log-likelihood ceilings: the split-half filler-model design}
\label{app:dll-ceiling}

For BehavScore, where the quantity of interest is the log-likelihood improvement ($\Delta LL$) a layer's surprisal contributes over a covariate-only baseline, a correlation-based ceiling does not directly apply. We instead adapt the split-half procedure \citet{timkey2026dissociation} use to contextualize their own filler-sentence regression models (their Appendix, Section~3B, ``Filler model goodness-of-fit''): we ask how much $\Delta LL$ a predictor could achieve in the best possible case, given only the same information available to a single word-level scalar like surprisal -- one value per item, no participant- or trial-level access.

We construct this best-case predictor directly from the data. For a given dataset, we randomly split the subject pool into two equal halves, $A$ and $B$. Within $A$, we compute the per-item mean response (averaged across subjects and, where relevant, electrodes), substitute it for the surprisal predictor in a mixed-effects model otherwise identical to our real full model, and fit it to the held-out half $B$. Comparing this model's log-likelihood against a baseline model (identical formula, predictor omitted) fit to the same $B$ data gives one estimate of the achievable $\Delta LL$. We swap the halves -- using $B$'s per-item means to predict $A$ -- and sum the $\Delta LL$ from both directions, repeating over 15 random splits and reporting the mean per-observation $\Delta LL$ as the ceiling.

Because the substitute predictor is an empirical group average rather than a noise-free oracle, this ceiling already absorbs the same between-subject averaging noise a real surprisal predictor faces, while staying agnostic to any particular theory of processing difficulty -- it is simply the most any single per-item scalar could explain, given the data's own reliability. We report a model's raw $\Delta LL$ alongside this ceiling and, where useful, as a percentage of it, exactly as \citeauthor{timkey2026dissociation} report their filler models' fit in absolute and relative terms. Two details carry over from our main fitting procedure: ceilings are computed out-of-sample, using the same item/chunk train-test split as the corresponding real analysis, and the ceiling model's random-effects structure mirrors the real model's for that dataset -- so the only thing distinguishing the two is the substitution of an empirical per-item mean for the model-derived surprisal.

\section{Implementation Details}
\label{app:implementation}

\paragraph{Data provenance.} All datasets were obtained from their public OpenNeuro or OSF repositories under the terms specified therein.

\paragraph{Feature extraction.} All language models are loaded in bfloat16 and evaluated as frozen feature extractors, with no gradient updates. For each word, hidden states are mean-pooled over its constituent subword tokens at every layer; unless otherwise noted (Section~3.1), a word's representation is computed with its full preceding sentence or discourse context in the forward pass, rather than in isolation. Surprisal at every layer is obtained via a logit-lens projection, applying each model's own unembedding matrix to the corresponding intermediate hidden state.

\paragraph{Ridge regression.} For every dataset, nested cross-validation selects the ridge penalty from a log-spaced grid via an inner search, with predictors standardized by a robust (median/IQR) scaler fit on the training fold only, and outer folds grouped so that no sentence, item, story, or run straddles the train/test boundary. The grid and fold count are tuned per dataset to its sample size and predictor dimensionality rather than fixed globally: Table~\ref{tab:ridge-config} lists the exact configuration for every dataset. We report mean out-of-fold Pearson and Spearman correlation across the outer folds, and sweep this procedure across every layer of every model to obtain a layerwise profile.

\input{sections/ridge_config_table}

\paragraph{Temporal response function (TRF) datasets.} For the three datasets with a temporal response rather than a single scalar per word -- Broderick, Podcast, and Fedorenko2016 -- the ridge encoder fits a lag-matrix TRF: one multi-output ridge regression predicts every time lag jointly from the same set of predictors, rather than one regression per lag. Broderick's EEG is downsampled to 32\,Hz and epoched from $-0.2$ to $1.0$\,s relative to word onset (38 lags); Podcast's ECoG uses the same 32\,Hz rate over a wider $-1.0$ to $1.0$\,s window (128 lags). Fedorenko2016 has no temporal axis: its intracranial recordings are already reduced to a single scalar response per (word, electrode) pair, so no lag-matrix step is needed there.

\paragraph{Podcast's pooled mixed-effects model.} In addition to its per-electrode ridge encoder, Podcast is also fit with a pooled linear mixed-effects model in which electrodes are nested within subject (Appendix~\ref{app:lme-formulas}), separately \emph{at each lag} of a $-0.2$ to $0.8$\,s, 32-lag window -- one model per (lag, LM layer) pair rather than one model for the whole epoch. Held-out partitions are formed from contiguous 30-word chunks of the continuous transcript, the direct word-index analogue of the chunk-based splits used for the fMRI datasets below.

\paragraph{fMRI encoding pipelines.} For Le Petit Prince and Natural Stories fMRI, word-level predictors are convolved with a canonical double-gamma HRF \citep{glover1999deconvolution}, implemented via \texttt{nilearn}, before ridge or mixed-effects fitting; both use $\mathrm{TR}=2$\,s and discard the first and last several volumes of each run for signal equilibration. Le Petit Prince additionally restricts voxels to the top 25\% by split-half reliability before averaging into ten bilateral parcels of the Fedorenko language-network atlas, and both its ridge and mixed-effects analyses are computed on the group-average BOLD signal rather than per subject, following the most-reliable-voxel selection and subject-averaging procedure of \citet{bonnasse2024fmri}. Natural Stories fMRI aggregates BOLD into 26 functional regions of interest (6 left-hemisphere language-network parcels, 20 bilateral multiple-demand parcels) per \citet{shain2022robust}. Tuckute's sentence-level design uses only the 1,000-sentence naturalistic condition for encoding and mixed-effects analyses; the two adversarially optimized conditions designed to drive or suppress the language network are evaluated separately, since only 2--3 of the 10 participants saw each of those sentences.

\paragraph{Linear mixed-effects models.} All LME models are fit in R via \texttt{lme4}. Where out-of-sample evaluation is used, held-out partitions are formed from contiguous chunks of the data (words, sentences, or short time windows depending on the dataset), shuffled with a fixed seed before assignment to train/held-out splits, so that both partitions sample the full range of each recording or corpus rather than, e.g., only its first half. If a model with the full random-effects structure specified in Appendix~\ref{app:lme-formulas} fails to converge, we follow the simplification procedure of \citet{barr2013random}: the correlation between random slopes is dropped first, and if convergence still fails, the slope itself is dropped, leaving a random-intercept-only term. This fallback is applied identically across every layer of a given model so that log-likelihood comparisons remain valid.

\section{Dependency Locality Theory Difficulty Stratification}
\label{app:dlt}

To ask whether a model's advantage is uniform across words or concentrated on the words independently known to be difficult for incremental parsing, we stratify every word by its Dependency Locality Theory (DLT) integration cost \citep{gibson1998linguistic,gibson2000dlt}. DLT cost quantifies the working-memory demand of attaching a word to its syntactic head as a function of how much intervening discourse material -- new discourse referents, chiefly nouns and verbs -- has to be held active since the head was last processed: a word whose head is many discourse referents back costs more to integrate than one whose head was just seen. We compute this cost per word from a dependency parse of each dataset's stimuli, following Gibson's original definition directly rather than an approximation, and split words into an \emph{easy} set (cost $=0$: heads immediately available) and a \emph{hard} set (cost $\geq 2$, or a stricter cost $\geq 3$--$5$ for the threshold sweep below), reusing the same BrainScore/BehavScore pipeline (Appendix~\ref{app:implementation}) but restricted to the corresponding word subset.

Figure~\ref{fig:pareto}B reports the hard set at cost $\geq 2$. We chose this as the headline threshold after inspecting the full grid from cost $\geq 1$ through $\geq 5$: corpus-wide hard-word counts fall off sharply as the threshold rises (several behavioral corpora drop to single-digit word counts by cost $\geq 4$--$5$, e.g.\ MECO, UCL Reading, and Provo), so a threshold has to be high enough to isolate genuinely hard integration cases but low enough that every dataset still contributes a usable sample. All datasets pool their hard-word items before averaging per model, so every hard-word comparison necessarily has less statistical power than the all-words comparison it is contrasted against, which is most visible in BehavScore's instability past cost $\geq 4$.

\paragraph{A caveat specific to the embedding track.} Best-layer selection confounds two things for BrainScore that it does not confound for BehavScore. First, the layer-count bias already noted in Section~3.3: a deeper model gets more attempts at a high held-out score by chance. Second, and specific to embeddings, hidden-state width scales with model size alongside depth, so a larger model's ridge regression is also higher-dimensional -- more regressors mechanically raise a ridge model's achievable in-sample fit, and while our nested cross-validation controls the regularization strength, it does not remove the advantage of having more (correlated) predictors to work with in the first place. Surprisal has neither confound: it is always a single scalar per layer regardless of a model's width, so the only thing best-layer selection can do for BehavScore is pick a better-fitting depth, not a wider one. This is also why we treat surprisal, not embeddings, as our primary comparison across model groups (Section~3.3) -- the embedding track's NeuralScore gap between Standard LLMs and the constrained groups should be interpreted with this capacity confound in mind, rather than as evidence of representational alignment alone.

\paragraph{BabyLM's last-layer embedding dissociation.} One pattern is specific to the last-layer embedding group means reported in Section~4.3 and is worth flagging on its own. At the last layer, BabyLM's mean embedding BehavScore (0.512) is higher than Standard LLM's (0.488; Welch's $t(13.0) = 2.87$, $p = 0.013$), while its mean embedding NeuralScore (0.105) is far lower than Standard LLM's (0.130; $t(16.1) = -5.19$, $p < 0.001$). In other words, a small model trained on a human-scale text budget can match or exceed a web-scale model at predicting reading times and fixations from its final-layer embedding, while remaining a substantially worse predictor of neural signal from that same embedding. This dissociation does not survive to the best layer (Table~\ref{tab:best-layer}: Standard LLM leads BabyLM on both scores once every layer is available), so it is a property of what a small, data-constrained model's \emph{last} representation looks like specifically, not of its representations in general -- consistent with the layerwise finding (Section~4.4) that BabyLM tends to peak before its final layer.
