\begin{abstract}
Do language models and human brains arrive at similar solutions to language processing? Two lines of work aim to make models more human-like: architectural inductive biases mirroring human cognitive limits, such as working-memory bottlenecks on attention, and training restricted to the linguistic input a child receives, as in the BabyLM Challenge. We evaluate whether models constrained by memory, data, or both predict human data better than unconstrained ones. We introduce \textit{\textbf{HumanScore-Language}}, a benchmark measuring how accurately model-derived surprisal and contextual embeddings predict reading times and eye movements alongside brain activity (fMRI, MEG, EEG, N400 event-related potentials, intracranial recordings) across eighteen datasets, each normalized by its own noise ceiling. Evaluating 118 models in a $2\times2$ factorial design, with mixed models that account for the dependence between related models and between datasets, we find that the two constraints are associated with behavioral and neural prediction in opposite directions for contextual embeddings. At matched size and perplexity, a developmental data budget is associated with better prediction of reading behavior, by about one between-model standard deviation, but not of neural responses, whereas memory constraints are associated with worse neural prediction and no reliable behavioral gain. Combining both constraints cancels the behavioral advantage, and within memory-limited architectures less training data does not improve behavioral prediction. Matched comparisons locate the neural penalty in forget-gate and distance-biased attention. These domain differences are tested directly and are robust to resampling model families, excluding datasets, and outlier treatment. Surprisal-based results are weaker and depend on datasets with unstable noise ceilings. Larger models predict neural responses better from embeddings, whereas at matched perplexity they predict reading behavior worse from surprisal. Complementary representational analyses show that small and constrained models remain competitive on behavioral measures, while neural rankings vary across evaluation methods. Together, these findings show that behavioral and neural alignment are distinct targets: a constraint that improves the prediction of human behavior need not improve the prediction of brain activity.
\end{abstract}
