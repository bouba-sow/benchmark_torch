Do humans and Large Language Models (LLMs) process language in a similar way? David Marr’s tri-level framework provides a principled lens to address this question by decomposing information-processing systems into three distinct levels (Figure 1A): the computational level (what is the goal of the computation?), the algorithmic level (what representations and step-by-step procedures are used?), and the implementational level (how are these steps physically realized?) \citep{marr1982vision}. To evaluate whether LLMs serve as meaningful models of human cognition, we can analyze both systems across these three levels. At the computational level, LLMs are primarily optimized for next-word prediction over massive text corpora. But is next-token prediction truly what the human brain does? While predictive processing plays a central role in human language \citep{hale2001probabilistic,levy2008expectation,smithlevy2013effect,shain2024large}, next-token prediction is at best a rough proxy; biological language processing is driven by broader communicative intent, pragmatic inferencing, multi-step predictive horizons, and complex social or goal-directed rewards \citep{bender-koller-2020-climbing,andreas-2022-language}. At the implementational level, clear physical and structural distinctions seem to exist. Although artificial neural networks drew early inspiration from neuroscience, modern LLMs have diverged significantly from their biological counterparts. Standard LLMs rely on global self-attention mechanisms across vast context windows \citep{vaswani2017attention}, uniform layerwise Transformer blocks that lack the dedicated modularity of the human cortical language network, non-local backpropagation rather than biologically plausible local learning rules (e.g., STDP), and training exposures spanning trillions of tokens -- orders of magnitude beyond a human lifetime \citep{gilkerson2017mapping,warstadt2023cfp}.

Given these differences at both the computational level (Level 1) and implementational level (Level 3), do LLMs and humans converge onto similar representations and processing steps at the algorithmic level (Level 2)? One can map out a spectrum of potential outcomes regarding their algorithmic alignment (Figure 1A). At the pessimistic extreme, these mismatches drive LLMs and human brains to adopt fundamentally different algorithmic steps to solve linguistic tasks. At the optimistic extreme -- aligned with the Platonic Representation Hypothesis \citep{huh2024platonic} -- shared environmental pressures and task demands force biological and artificial systems to converge onto the same underlying representations and algorithms despite their structural differences. Whether LLMs and human brains actually converge at the algorithmic level remains an open empirical question.

Unlike engineering goals focused purely on task performance, cognitive scientists and neuroscientists aim to build models that emulate and explain human-like cognition. To guarantee that artificial models converge on mechanisms closer to human language processing at the algorithmic level, researchers have begun incorporating explicit biological and developmental constraints. On the architectural side, researchers introduce human cognitive limitations, such as working memory capacity bottlenecks \citep{lewisvasishth2005activation}, recurrent processing dynamics \citep{michaelov2024revenge}, or localized attention structures \citep{devarda2024locally,kuribayashi2022context}. On the data side, initiatives such as the BabyLM Challenge restrict training corpora to developmentally realistic, child-scale linguistic input (~10M to 100M words) rather than web-scale text (Figure 1B; \citealp{warstadt2023cfp,warstadt2023findings}). However, whether these human-like constraints actually succeed in driving algorithmic similarity remains an open question. It is entirely unclear if introducing such inductive biases genuinely aligns model mechanisms with human processing, or if standard, unconstrained LLMs already achieve comparable algorithmic convergence simply through task-scale optimization.

How can we empirically determine which algorithms are used by humans and LLMs during language processing? In humans, algorithmic steps are empirically probed through behavioral and neural measures (Figure 1B). Specifically, in psycholinguistics, behavioral measures typically include error rates, error types, self-paced reading times, and eye-tracking fixations \citep{futrell2021natural,frank2013reading,luke2018provo,siegelman2022meco}. These offer insights into processing difficulty and moment-by-moment cognitive load. In neuroscience, algorithmic dynamics are captured across complementary spatio-temporal scales: functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) provides whole-brain spatial coverage of, e.g., representational similarity \citep{schrimpf2021neural}; Electroencephalography (EEG) tracks precise temporal dynamics, such as the N400 and P600 event-related potentials \citep{michaelov2024strong,federmeier2007multiple}; and intracranial electrophysiology (e.g., ECoG) provides joint spatial precision and millisecond-level signal resolution directly from cortical circuits \citep{schrimpf2021neural}. Combined, these multi-modal measurements provide the means to evaluate the neural representations and computations underlying language processing. In artificial neural networks, internal mechanisms can be analyzed directly via model interpretability methods; however, given the immense complexity of these black-box representations, interpreting them in isolation remains challenging. Here, rather than relying on internal model interpretability, we test for algorithmic convergence by examining whether LLM-derived metrics, such as token surprisal and internal contextual embeddings, can accurately predict behavioral and neural measurements recorded from humans. Evaluating this predictive power across different model classes allows us to answer the central question of this study: whether humans and LLMs converge on similar algorithmic solutions, and in particular, whether human-constrained models achieve significantly greater convergence than standard LLMs.

We systematically test and contrast four groups of models in their capacity to predict human behavioral and neural responses (Figure 1C), crossing the architectural and data constraints above into a $2\times2$ design: (1) memory-constrained architectures trained at web scale, (2) the same memory-constrained architectures trained on BabyLM-scale data, (3) standard architectures trained on BabyLM-scale data, and (4) standard, off-the-shelf LLMs with neither constraint. By evaluating these four groups against our multi-modal human benchmark, we investigate whether introducing human-like constraints -- in network architecture, training data size, or both -- is essential for achieving genuine algorithmic alignment between artificial neural networks and the human brain, and whether the two kinds of constraint compound or substitute for one another.

Across seventeen behavioral and neural datasets and over a hundred evaluated models, we find no evidence that either constraint helps: on every dataset modality we test, the Pareto front trading off neural and behavioral predictive power is occupied exclusively by standard, unconstrained LLMs, and this holds whether we look at all words or specifically at the syntactically hardest ones. Restricting a model's attention to nearby context lowers its alignment with human data outright, regardless of whether it is trained at web scale or human scale; restricting training data alone, while it leaves a standard architecture intact, still does not produce a model that outperforms its web-scale counterpart on neural predictivity, though it comes far closer on behavioral predictivity than the architecturally constrained models do. Scale itself keeps paying off within the unconstrained group, across nearly every modality we test, in a way it does not for the memory-constrained groups. Taken together, these results are the pattern the Platonic Representation Hypothesis predicts, and not the pattern the human-constraint hypothesis predicts: task-scale optimization on its own, without any explicit human-inspired inductive bias, appears to be doing more of the work of aligning a model with human language processing than the biases themselves are.
