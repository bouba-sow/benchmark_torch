\section{Related Literature}

A growing body of work has asked whether LLM representations line up with human brain activity and behavior during language processing. Model-derived features -- most often next-word surprisal or hidden-state embeddings -- have been shown to predict fMRI and MEG responses during naturalistic listening and reading \citep{schrimpf2021neural,caucheteux2022brains,caucheteux2023evidence}, to explain variance in fMRI/ECoG recordings beyond what simpler linguistic features capture \citep{toneva2019interpreting}, and to track a hierarchy of predictive signals across the cortical language network \citep{caucheteux2023evidence}. These correlations hold up even for models trained on child-scale corpora \citep{hosseini2024artificial} and can be strengthened through instruction tuning \citep{aw2024instructiontuning}, and recent work has found that recurrent architectures match or exceed Transformers on several of these comprehension metrics despite their very different implementation \citep{michaelov2024revenge}. A parallel line of work has probed the internal computations of LLMs directly and reported that intermediate layers already encode next-token distributions in a way that resembles human online processing \citep{kuribayashi2025humanlike}, and that reading times can be decoded from hidden states even when the model was never trained to predict them \citep{tsipidi2026probing}. Taken together, these findings motivate the Platonic Representation Hypothesis \citep{huh2024platonic}: the idea that models trained on different objectives, architectures, and data eventually settle into overlapping representational geometry simply because they are all being pushed to solve related tasks over similar sensory and linguistic statistics.

If shared task pressure is sufficient to produce convergence, why constrain a model at all? One answer, pursued mostly in psycholinguistics, is that human sentence processing operates under resource limits that standard LLMs do not share, and that building those limits in should make a model behave more like a human reader even if it does not change its raw task performance. On the architecture side, this has taken the form of restricting the attention mechanism itself: single-head or otherwise capacity-limited attention \citep{timkey2023limited}, distance-based decay that biases attention toward nearby tokens \citep{devarda2024locally,clark2025linear,thamma2025humanlike}, content-sensitive forget gates \citep{lin2025forgetting} or stick-breaking attention \citep{tan2025scaling}, and various memory-bounded or resource-rational formulations of the encoding process \citep{hahn2022resourcerational,lieder2019resourcerational,xu2026memory,xufutrell2026strategic,rasmussen2017leftcorner,keshev2025working}. These architectural choices are usually motivated by classic capacity-limited theories of sentence processing, such as activation-based memory retrieval \citep{lewisvasishth2005activation} and lossy-context surprisal \citep{futrell2020lossy}. On the data side, the BabyLM Challenge takes the opposite route and leaves the architecture alone but restricts training exposure to the 10-100 million words a child hears by a given age, well below the token budgets of standard LLMs \citep{warstadt2023cfp,warstadt2023findings,hu2024secondbabylm}, building on earlier child-directed-language pretraining work \citep{huebner2021babyberta}. Whether either of these constraints actually buys anything in terms of fit to human data is contested: some studies report that smaller, more human-scale models fit reading times better \citep{wilcox2024bigger,ohschuler2023twobillion}, while others find the opposite, or find that fit to human data and standard language-modeling quality trade off in ways that are not well understood \citep{ohschuler2023why,kuribayashi2021lower}.

A separate and more technical literature asks how, exactly, one should use an LLM's internal state to predict a given human measurement, largely independent of whether the model itself is human-like. Surprisal remains the dominant predictor of reading times and self-paced reading data \citep{goodkind2018predictive,wilcox2020predictive,wilcox2023testing}, with psychometric predictive power now benchmarked systematically across model families and sizes \citep{kuribayashi2024psychometric}. Because surprisal is normally read off a model's final output layer, several groups have instead probed intermediate layers directly -- via the logit lens \citep{logitlens2020} or the tuned lens \citep{belrose2023tuned} -- to ask which stage of processing best matches human timing \citep{tenney2019bert}; \citet{kuribayashi2025humanlike} report that this intermediate-layer surprisal tracks human reading times increasingly well with depth on several behavioral datasets, well before the final layer. Related work has generalized what counts as an "anticipation" signal in online processing more broadly \citep{giulianelli2024generalized,opedal2024role}.

Closely related and concurrent with our own work, \citet{devarda2026behavioral} compare a low-dimensional effort predictor (surprisal, frequency, and word length) against high-dimensional final-layer embeddings across eight behavioral and five neural datasets, several of which overlap with ours (Natural Stories, Provo, MECO, ZuCo, Tuckute, UCL). They report that behavioral measures are well explained by the low-dimensional effort predictor alone, with embeddings adding little, whereas brain responses require the richer embedding space, and that this split coincides with opposite scaling trends within the GPT-2 family -- smaller models fit behavior better, larger models fit the brain better. Their question is largely orthogonal to ours: they hold the model class fixed (a single GPT-2 family) and ask which \emph{predictor} -- effort features or embeddings -- best explains each modality, whereas we hold the predictor fixed (surprisal or full embeddings, applied identically to every model) and ask which \emph{model class} -- architecturally or developmentally constrained versus standard -- best explains the data, across a substantially larger sweep of models, model families, and neural/behavioral datasets.

Our study differs from this literature along two axes at once. First, rather than picking a side in the constrained-versus-standard debate, we treat it as an empirical question and put memory-constrained architectures, BabyLM-trained models, and standard off-the-shelf LLMs through the exact same evaluation pipeline, so that any advantage for the "human-like" models has to show up as a direct, apples-to-apples effect rather than being read off separate papers with separate datasets and separate metrics. Second, we do this at a scale that, to our knowledge, has not been attempted for this specific comparison: rather than one behavioral corpus or one neuroimaging dataset, we pool self-paced reading and eye-tracking corpora together with fMRI, EEG/ERP, and intracranial recordings, so that a claim of algorithmic convergence (or its absence) is not an artifact of any single modality, measure, or population.
