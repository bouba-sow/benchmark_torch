\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Discussion}

Our results show that human-inspired constraints are associated with behavioral and neural alignment in different ways, rather than improving both. For contextual embeddings, a developmental data budget is associated with better behavioral prediction but not neural prediction, while memory constraints are associated with worse neural prediction and no reliable behavioral gain; the joint model shows that both differences between domains are reliable. Combining both constraints cancels the behavioral advantage of the data budget, and within memory-limited architectures, training on less data does not improve behavioral prediction. The data advantage is therefore a contrast between BabyLM-trained standard architectures and web-scale LLMs rather than an effect of the amount of data alone, and the neural memory penalty is concentrated in forget-gate and distance-biased attention rather than shared by all memory mechanisms. For surprisal, the behavioral data advantage is weaker and the neural constraint coefficients depend on datasets with unstable noise ceilings, so we do not interpret them. These findings suggest that predicting human reading behavior and neural responses places different demands on language models.

Model scale further highlights this dissociation. Larger models achieve higher neural prediction scores from embeddings, consistent with scaling accounts of representational convergence \citep{huh2024platonic,shain2024large}. This association is weaker once dependence between models is modeled, and it should be interpreted with caution: \citet{alkhamissi-etal-2025-language} find that model size is not a reliable predictor of brain alignment once the number of features is controlled, and our ridge encoders have more features for larger models. They also find that alignment with the language network saturates early in training, and that brain alignment stops tracking next-word prediction and behavioral alignment once models surpass human proficiency, with a negative correlation between brain and behavioral alignment late in training for larger models. This divergence parallels the opposite size associations we observe for neural embedding prediction and behavioral surprisal prediction, although our models differ in size and architecture rather than in training stage. However, larger size is negatively associated with behavioral surprisal prediction after controlling for perplexity, consistent with previous reports that scaling does not necessarily improve behavioral alignment \citep{ohschuler2023why,ohschuler2023twobillion,kuribayashi2021lower,devarda2026behavioral}. One possibility is that smaller or constrained models retain information particularly relevant to human processing difficulty, whereas larger models better predict neural responses. However, these associations do not establish an underlying mechanism.

Differences across evaluation methods also qualify model rankings. Direct encoding, RSA, and RDM regression capture distinct aspects of correspondence and do not always favor the same models. In particular, memory-constrained models occupy the neural end of the surprisal RDM-regression frontier despite their lower direct neural prediction scores, although neural scores on that frontier are close to or below the lexical baseline, so this reflects a ranking rather than good absolute prediction. Together, these results suggest that neither model scale nor a single evaluation metric provides a complete characterization of human-language alignment.

\subsection{Limitations}

Our comparisons are observational and cannot establish causal effects of memory or data constraints. Model families differ in architecture, training objectives, and corpora, and the constraint groups have limited overlap in size and perplexity. Consequently, regression adjustment may involve extrapolation. We model the dependence between models of one lineage, between scores of one model, and between units of one corpus with random effects, and we check the conclusions with a lineage cluster bootstrap and several exclusion and outlier analyses. These checks matter: a specification with only a dataset random intercept yields much smaller standard errors, and several associations it suggests, including a behavioral advantage of memory constraints and all neural surprisal constraint effects, do not survive them. Each constrained group still contains only five or six lineages, so even the robust associations rest on a small number of independent architectures and training pipelines.

Our evaluation measures also have different assumptions and coverage. Embedding encoders do not explicitly control for lexical variables, unlike surprisal and RDM-regression analyses. Noise-ceiling estimates have finite reliability, and normalized neural scores can vary substantially across datasets. Moreover, representational analyses cover fewer neural datasets, and differences between evaluation methods cannot be attributed to a single factor.

Finally, developmental data restrictions and the tested memory mechanisms capture only limited aspects of human language learning and cognition. Our conclusions are restricted to the evaluated model families, final-layer representations, and predominantly English datasets with limited participant or recording coverage. Most models remain below the estimated noise ceilings, leaving substantial room for improvement. Predictive correspondence alone does not establish shared representations or computational mechanisms.

Overall, a developmental data budget is associated with better prediction of human reading behavior without better prediction of neural responses, and memory constraints with worse neural prediction, emphasizing the importance of evaluating both targets with complementary measures.
